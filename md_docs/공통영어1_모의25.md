**1.** 다음 글의 **주제**로 가장 적절한 것은? \[T-01·T2\]

For most of human history, doing one thing at a time was not a virtue but a necessity. The modern workplace, however, treats attention as if it were infinitely divisible—an assumption that neuroscience has steadily undermined. When we believe we are multitasking, what the brain is actually doing is switching rapidly between separate tasks, and each switch carries a hidden toll. Researchers who study this cost describe it as "attention residue," a fragment of the previous task that lingers and clouds the next one. Studies suggest that people who juggle several streams of information perform worse on tests of memory and concentration than those who focus on a single task, even when they feel more productive. In a culture that prizes constant busyness, the rarest and most valuable skill may be the willingness to do less, more deeply.

**2.** 다음 글의 **제목**으로 가장 적절한 것은? \[T-01·T2\]

On a summer evening, a city can be several degrees warmer than the countryside around it, a phenomenon known as the urban heat island. The cause is largely material: concrete and asphalt absorb heat all day and release it slowly at night, while glass and steel trap warmth between buildings. Cars, air conditioners, and crowds add still more. The effect is not trivial; it raises energy use, worsens air quality, and endangers the elderly during heat waves. Fortunately, the remedies are well understood. Planting trees, painting roofs white, and creating parks can all cool a city measurably, turning a problem of design into an opportunity for it.

**5.** 다음 글의 내용과 **일치하지 않는** 것은? \[T-02·T2\]

When a honeybee discovers a rich source of food, it returns to the hive and performs a remarkable "waggle dance" to tell the others where to go. By moving in a figure-eight pattern and waggling its body, the bee encodes two crucial pieces of information: the direction of the food relative to the sun, and its distance from the hive. The longer the waggle, the farther away the food lies. Other bees follow closely, reading the message through touch and vibration in the dark hive. This dance is one of the few known examples of a non-human animal communicating abstract information about a distant place.

① The waggle dance conveys both direction and distance. ② A longer waggle indicates food that is farther away. ③ Bees interpret the dance through touch and vibration. ④ The dance communicates the location of a distant food source. ⑤ The direction is encoded relative to the hive entrance.
